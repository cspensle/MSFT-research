Hi, I'm Colin Spensley, and this is Video 2 for my Microsoft valuation project. It describes how my project works and how I checked it. First, the README.md file identifies the investment decision, data sources, setup commands, and model limitations. The main files include proforma.py, workbench.py, dcf.py, and app.py, which I will discuss later.

If we open proforma.py, it has the base assumptions. It projects five years of linked income statements, balance sheets, and cash flows. Those results feed into workbench.py, where the model calculates free cash flow to the firm and discounts it to estimate enterprise value. It then adds specified nonoperating cash, deducts borrowing and finance-lease liabilities, and divides equity value by diluted shares. app.py displays the results and lets a user change selected assumptions. I will discuss the app in Video 3.

Free cash flow starts with operating profit after tax. The model adds back noncash depreciation and stock compensation. It then accounts for cash capital spending, finance-lease additions, and changes in operating working capital. Lease additions represent infrastructure investment in the cash-flow calculation. The lease liability is also included in the enterprise-to-equity bridge. The model is designed to avoid deducting the same lease principal twice.

In workbench.py, the checks test whether the balance sheet balances, forecast cash ties to the cash-flow calculation, cash stays above the model's minimum, diluted shares are positive, and terminal growth stays below WACC. Automated tests check the valuation direction: increasing WACC should lower value, while increasing valid terminal growth should raise it. All five tests pass. This does not mean that my valuation or forecast assumptions are necessarily correct; it means that the model passes those tests. proforma.py and workbench.py show their outputs, and test_workbench.py contains the five tests.

I also ran a locked input test. I selected annual cash capital spending because I wanted to see what would happen if cash spending went down as AI spending slowed. I recorded the prediction, timestamp, and pre-run repository commit before running it. Holding all other assumptions constant, I changed FY2030 cash capital spending from $155 billion to $125 billion and FY2031 from $160 billion to $120 billion. The $238.17 per-share estimate increased by $46.91, matching my prediction that value would rise.

The estimate remained below the saved market price, so my recommendation stayed watch/defer. This is a controlled sensitivity test, not evidence that Microsoft's spending will actually fall. If it did, other things might also have to change before my valuation would support a buy decision.
